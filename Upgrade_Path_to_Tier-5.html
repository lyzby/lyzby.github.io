Upgrade path to Tier 5.

Tier 5 is no longer a charging shelf with extras. It is a real household power system: kilowatts of PV, a 48 V battery, a hybrid inverter and fixed AC circuits. The project’s working reference is roughly 2.4 kWp of panels, 5–15 kWh of LiFePO₄ storage and a 5–6.3 kW inverter — while keeping the original Tier-2 DC system as a small independent backup.

The threshold and the practical design are not the same thing.

The Tier-5 shorthand is 2 kW of capacity and 8.2 kWh/day. Three 666 W panels or five 400 W panels are almost exactly 2.0 kWp and therefore satisfy the power threshold on paper. But at 5 peak-sun-hours and a conservative 75% net system yield, 2.0 kWp produces only about 7.5 kWh/day. The project therefore treats ~2.4 kWp as a more realistic starting array.

MINIMUM

3 × 666 W or 5 × 400 W

About 2.0 kWp. This is the useful lower boundary for thinking about Tier 5, but it leaves little energy margin after real-world losses.

WORKING TARGET

4 × 600 W or 6 × 400 W

About 2.4 kWp. At 5 peak-sun-hours and 75% net yield, that is about 9 kWh/day — enough to clear the 8.2 kWh shorthand on a typical day.

MORE MARGIN

4 × 666 W or similar

About 2.66 kWp. More PV is often cheaper than more battery and makes cooking, pumping and cooling easier to schedule directly into daylight.

Panel count is easy. String design is not.

At this scale, module wattage alone is not enough. Each string must sit inside the chosen hybrid inverter’s MPPT voltage window, remain below its maximum open-circuit voltage in cool weather, and remain below its per-input current limit. Different panel types should normally be kept on separate MPPT inputs.

Do not infer stringing from this table. “Five 400 W panels” describes total PV capacity, not a universal 3+2 wiring recipe. A two-panel string can fall below the minimum MPPT voltage of some high-voltage hybrids. The final series/parallel layout must be designed around the exact inverter datasheet and local temperature range.

48 V storage becomes the organizing bus.

Tier 5 is where a 48 V-class battery starts to make obvious electrical sense. At several kilowatts, a 24 V battery would require very high current; 48 V keeps conductors, switches and inverter input current manageable. The project is using a 5 / 10 / 15 kWh storage ladder rather than pretending there is one universally correct battery size.

5 kWh

Minimum practical start

Enough for several hours of important evening loads if cooking, pumping and other heavy work are moved into daylight. It is not a full day of Tier-5 energy.

10 kWh

Working standard

Roughly comparable to the Tier-5 daily-energy threshold and much more comfortable for refrigeration, evening loads and an overnight reserve.

15 kWh

High-margin household

More useful where clouds are common, nighttime cooling matters, or the grid is unreliable enough that the battery is doing backup duty as well as solar shifting.

The Tier-3 battery may not become part of the 48 V bank. A 12.8 V or 25.6 V pack should not simply be series-stacked with unrelated batteries. It can remain useful on a dedicated DC circuit, be reassigned to refrigeration or lighting backup, or be resold. “Upgradeable” should mean preserving value, not forcing every earlier component into one electrical bus.

At Tier 5, schedule matters almost as much as capacity.

Several household loads draw kilowatts but only briefly. Running them while the sun is strong can keep both the battery and the inverter smaller. The load list below is illustrative; actual priorities will differ enormously among households.

Tier 5 also means fixed AC wiring. Unlike the entry system, this rung needs a distribution board, over-current protection, residual-current protection where applicable, earthing, correctly sized branch circuits and installation to the local electrical standard. That is a fundamentally different field skill from mounting a panel and charging shelf.

Working hardware economics.

The project’s present Tier-5 cost model is built around commodity PV, a low-cost 48 V hybrid inverter and modular 5 kWh-class LiFePO₄ batteries. It is closer to the LagosVerter economics than to conventional North American rooftop-solar pricing. These are working targets, not supplier quotations.

Where this rung stands.

The Tier-5 page is an architecture and economics study. The purpose is not to pretend that every Tier-2 household will buy this system. It is to show that the first rung can lead somewhere substantial without making the first installation unnecessarily expensive.

Working principles

  • About 2.4 kWp is a more useful starting target than the bare 2.0 kW threshold.
  • 48 V LiFePO₄ storage in a 5 / 10 / 15 kWh ladder.
  • 5–6.3 kW hybrid inverter with MPPT inputs appropriate to the actual panel strings.
  • Keep different panel types on separate MPPT inputs where practical.
  • Schedule cooking, pumping and flexible work into daylight.
  • Keep the original Tier-2 charging shelf as independent low-power backup.

Still open

  • Country-specific 230 V / 50 Hz hybrid inverter platform and protection scheme.
  • Best module size: 400 W, 600 W, 666 W or another commodity class at deployment time.
  • Installer training for fixed AC wiring and earthing.
  • Landed cost of 5 kWh battery modules at very large volume.
  • How much Tier-3 equipment is retained, reassigned or resold during the 48 V transition.
