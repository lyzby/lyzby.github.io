Upgrade path to Tier 3.

Keep the original ~100 W panel, Distributor V1 and rechargeable appliances. Add a larger PV circuit — one or more 400 W-class panels — and enough shared storage to carry useful loads into the evening. The upgrade is additive: the entry system remains a working DC subsystem rather than becoming stranded equipment.

What changes at Tier 3.

The Tier-2 system deliberately avoids a central battery. Tier 3 is the point where a shared battery begins to earn its keep: longer fan use, an evening television, laptop or tool charging, and — with careful appliance choice — small refrigeration. The rechargeable lights, radio, phone and power banks still keep their own batteries and still charge from the original distributor.

ONE PANEL

400 W can cross the energy threshold

As a planning example, 400 W × 5 peak-sun-hours = 2.0 kWh gross. At a deliberately conservative 75% system yield, that is about 1.5 kWh/day — above the 1.0 kWh Tier-3 shorthand on an ordinary sunny day.

MARGIN

600–800 W is a more forgiving system

More PV does not merely add appliances. It gives the household cloudy-day margin, faster battery recovery after a poor day, and more daytime-direct energy for pumps, tools, food heating or refrigeration.

STORAGE

About 1–2.5 kWh changes the evening

A 1.28 kWh LiFePO₄ battery is a plausible first shared store. A 2.56 kWh pack provides much more overnight margin without turning the system into a full Tier-5 installation.

Keeping the original distributor independent is deliberate. A fault, flat battery or failed inverter on the larger system should not take away basic lighting and phone charging.

Two practical ways to make the step.

A single modern 400 W-class panel is enough PV for a minimal Tier-3 experiment. Two panels make a cleaner 24 V growth path and provide much better weather margin. Exact panel voltage, MPPT range and battery charge voltage must be checked together; panel wattage alone does not determine compatibility.

One-panel / 24 V caution. A typical 400 W module may operate around 30–32 V at maximum power and lower when hot. A 25.6 V LiFePO₄ battery may need roughly 28–29 V near the top of charge. That leaves too little headroom for many ordinary buck-type MPPT controllers. With one panel, a 12.8 V battery is the simple route; with two panels, a series string gives a 24 V controller much more voltage margin.

Do not feed the larger panel directly into Distributor V1. V1 is being qualified around a nominal-12 V ~100 W panel, roughly 18 V at maximum power and ~22 V open circuit. A 400 W-class module commonly has a substantially higher open-circuit voltage. The larger PV circuit needs its own controller.

Working cost envelope.

The point of Tier 3 is not to reproduce a conventional expensive solar-home-system package. The project is deliberately testing whether commodity panels, increasingly inexpensive LiFePO₄ storage and simple power electronics can keep the next rung in the few-hundred-dollar range. These are planning targets, not quotations.

Where this rung stands.

Tier 3 is a design study, not yet a field-tested product. Its main purpose today is to ensure the entry system does not paint the household into a corner.

Working principles

  • Keep the original ~100 W panel and Distributor V1 in service.
  • Add the larger PV on a separate controlled circuit.
  • Use LiFePO₄ for shared storage.
  • One 400 W panel can be a minimal step; 600–800 W is a more robust household system.
  • Prefer efficient DC loads; add a small inverter only for appliances that actually require AC.

Still to demonstrate

  • Actual refrigerator consumption in hot households.
  • Best low-cost 12 V / 24 V controller or hybrid platform.
  • Battery cycle life at the price points the project requires.
  • Whether households prefer one shared battery or several portable stores.
  • Real upgrade financing after Tier-2 repayment history exists.
